# 2022: The Dark Age of Architecture Work

## Looking Back

From my point of view, 2022 was a dark age for architecture work. It was not a dark age because architects lacked skill or ambition. It felt dark because the knowledge expected of us had reached a peak, while the assistants that can now help us explore, organize, and challenge ideas were not yet part of many teams' everyday workflow.

An architect was expected to understand far more than systems and diagrams. We had to move between business goals, application design, cloud platforms, data, integration, security, reliability, cost, governance, and operations. We were asked to know enough about each area to identify dependencies, ask the right questions, evaluate options, and explain trade-offs to people with very different perspectives.

The hard part was not simply acquiring information. It was carrying all of it at once.

## The Weight of Knowing Enough

Architecture work often starts with incomplete context. A request arrives as a business goal, a list of constraints, a vendor proposal, or a problem that has already crossed several teams. The architect has to turn that material into a coherent view of the system and its risks.

That work means finding the missing facts, checking assumptions, comparing products, reading technical documentation, understanding existing systems, and preparing decisions that other people can act on. It also means keeping track of what is known, what is uncertain, and what needs validation.

Without a readily available AI collaborator, much of this exploration depended on individual effort: searching across sources, switching between topics, taking notes, drafting alternatives, and repeatedly reconstructing context. The work could feel less like architecture and more like trying to hold an entire technology landscape in one person's head.

The result was not always better architecture. Sometimes the pressure to know everything made it harder to ask basic questions, expose uncertainty, or spend enough time thinking about the actual problem.

## A Change in the Way We Work

AI assistants began to change this experience by making parts of the thinking process easier to start and easier to repeat. They can help summarize unfamiliar material, compare options, explain a concept at different levels, organize workshop notes, draft an initial architecture narrative, or turn a broad question into a list of concrete investigations.

That does not mean architecture became automatic. An assistant can produce a confident answer that is incomplete, outdated, or wrong for the system in front of us. It does not own the business outcome, know every organizational constraint, or accept responsibility for a decision.

The value is in having a collaborator for exploration, not an authority to obey. AI can reduce the friction of getting from a blank page to a set of possibilities. The architect still has to test those possibilities against evidence, context, and the consequences of being wrong.

## What AI Can Give Back to Architects

Used thoughtfully, AI assistance can create more room for the work that needs human judgment:

- Ask sharper questions during discovery instead of trying to appear to know everything already.
- Explore unfamiliar technologies quickly, then verify important details in primary sources.
- Compare alternatives using explicit criteria such as security, operability, cost, and fit.
- Turn scattered notes into decision records, diagrams, risks, and follow-up actions.
- Make complex technical ideas easier to discuss with business and operations teams.
- Challenge a proposed design by asking what assumptions, failure modes, or dependencies it may have missed.

These are starting points. The architect must still validate the output, protect sensitive information, and make clear where a statement is a fact, an assumption, or a recommendation.

## The Role Is Changing, Not Disappearing

If 2022 felt like the peak of required knowledge, the answer is not for every architect to memorize even more. The opportunity is to build a better way of working: combine experience, reliable sources, collaboration across teams, and AI tools that help us reason through complexity.

Architecture has always been more than knowing product features or drawing boxes. It is the practice of connecting intent to implementation, making trade-offs visible, and helping people make decisions under uncertainty. AI can assist with the research and communication around that work, but it cannot replace responsibility for the decision.

## A More Hopeful View

I remember 2022 as a demanding moment: a time when the expected breadth of knowledge felt enormous and the tools available to help us work through it felt limited. That is my perspective, not a claim that every architect experienced the same year or that useful AI tools did not exist at all.

What has changed is the possibility of working with an assistant while we learn, design, and communicate. The goal is not to outsource architectural thinking. It is to make room for more of it: more curiosity, better questions, clearer options, and decisions grounded in evidence and human judgment.
